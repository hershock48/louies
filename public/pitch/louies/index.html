The bakery is world class. The website is the only part of it that isn’t.

Everything above is a symptom, and none of it is the real finding. The real finding is the gap. Walk into 144 W. Michigan at 5:30 in the morning and you get the best bakery anywhere. We will say that plainly, because seventy-four years of Marshall lining up at one counter says it with us, and because people who moved away still pay to have a box of it sent across the country. Type your name into a browser and you get a site that says closed, staffed by people who do not exist. The quality on the screen has never once matched the quality in the case. A bakery this good deserves a website built to its own standard, and closing that gap is the entire reason this letter exists.

Why did the gap happen? Not neglect of the craft, plainly. A rented website that is awkward to change, so for six years nobody changed it.

Section two

The part that matters most.

What you have now

Your site runs on a Mopro subscription. Their link sits in your footer. You pay monthly for a website that froze in 2019, and if you ever stop paying, there is nothing to keep: the platform, the templates and the hosting are theirs.

What we would do instead

You own it. All of it.

When the build fee is paid, the site is yours in writing: design, source code, content, accounts. The domain is registered in your name. Month to month after launch, thirty days' notice, no penalty. If you ever leave, you take everything with you, working.

And the site knows what day it is

The stale vacation banner is not a typo somebody forgot. It is what happens when opening hours are a sentence a person has to remember to edit. On the site we built, open and closed are computed from the clock in Marshall. A vacation is one dated line; when the date passes, the site moves on by itself. The bug on your homepage today is not fixed on the new site. It is impossible.

Three questions worth asking any web vendor

1. If we stop paying you, what do we keep? 2. Can we see the price of every added service before it is added? 3. Whose name is the domain registered in? Our answers are: everything, yes, and yours. Ask Mopro the same three.

What is in it

A board that answers "what's in the case today." Your rotation already has rules: chocolate drops on Wednesdays, lemon jelly on Saturdays, eclairs in the cold months, photo cookies on two days' notice. The site knows them all, so the homepage says what is in the case this morning without anyone updating anything, ever.

A menu people can use. All sixty-one things you make, searchable, with a one-tap "in the case today" filter, and it prints onto four clean pages for the church-hall and office orders. One price per line, matching what Square charges at the counter, and changing a price is a text message to us, not a support ticket.

Your story, told with real people. Louie, 1952, the carousel oven that never fully goes off, and Jason's own words. The invented staff are gone.

Your reviews, on your site. "Best Damn Doughnuts Ever" belongs on your homepage, not three clicks away on someone else's.

An order form that works at 9pm. Your counter closes at 3. The form takes the dozen, the pie order and the photo-cookie request while you sleep, and it reaches you by email.

And the hat. Open the demo and watch your logo for a second. We think Louie would have smiled.

Built to a standard, and here is how we know

None of this is a mockup. It is a working site, measured rather than described.

  • About two seconds to first paint on a phone, measured on a deliberately bad connection with a slow processor, which is the profile Google grades a site on.
  • Zero accessibility failures at four screen sizes, checked with the standard auditing tool rather than by eye. Plenty of your customers are reading a phone at arm's length.
  • It still works with JavaScript switched off, on an old phone, on the bakery's own wifi. All sixty-one items, still there.
  • Nothing rented on top. No plugin subscriptions, no per-feature upsell, no page-builder licence.

The part where the shipping money is

You already ship two ways: the boxes you pack yourself, and your Goldbelly listing. Keep Goldbelly. Introducing you to somebody in Arizona who has never heard of Marshall is the one thing a marketplace does that a website cannot.

But a marketplace charges for that introduction on every order, forever, including the tenth order from the customer who now knows your name and would have typed your address in. Goldbelly does not publish that number; you know yours. Put it next to this one: on your own site a card payment costs about 2.9% plus 30 cents, the same order of money you already hand Square at the counter, and nothing else. No commission on the repeat order. And the buyer's email address is yours, which is the whole difference between a customer and a stranger who bought something once.

Here is the size of it, in your own numbers. Your Goldbelly listing sells five things, and a dozen Classic Nut Rolls is $75.95 delivered. That same dozen is roughly $23 at the prices on your menu. The difference is the tin, the freight and the marketplace's share, and today not one dollar of it is yours to set. Sell that dozen from your own page and whatever is left after the box and the truck is a decision you get to make: keep it, or hand some back and win the second order.

The demand is not theoretical either. The two reviews on that listing are the same customer in Tucson, Arizona, buying twice, and what she wrote about is the packing: "Not one cookie broke!" Somebody 1,700 miles away is a repeat customer of a bakery whose own website does not mention that it ships.

You already have a cart on your own site. It is the page quoting April. That is phase two, and it is a real phase, not a bolt-on: pick the box, pay, and the label prints in the back. The box page in the demo already lists all five, tins and fritters and the pumpkin cookies that come back in the fall. Quoted separately, when you want it, and never added to your bill before you say so.
